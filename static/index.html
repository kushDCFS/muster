Why there is no "lives saved" number here

The most common way to make a tool like this look impressive is to convert a coverage improvement into lives saved, then multiply by a Value of Statistical Life (~$13M each) to produce an enormous dollar figure. We deliberately don't.

A systematic review of 115 studies and 691,056 patients found insufficient evidence linking EMS response time to survival at the margins this product operates in. Building a headline number on a causal link the literature doesn't support would be exactly the kind of result this project's own FINDINGS.md was written to reject. Every figure on this page is a cost or workload number that stands without that assumption.

Why coverage gap = crew assembly, not truck-busy

An earlier version of this model treated "coverage gap" as urban queueing theory: P(the truck is already out on another call), via an Erlang-C approximation. That's the wrong mechanism for most rural systems — rural ambulance utilization typically runs 5–15% (vs. 30–50% urban targets), so simultaneous-call collisions are rare by construction. The rural EMS literature documents a different, bigger failure mode: a crew simply doesn't form for the tone-out, because 70–74% of rural EMS providers aren't full-time EMS and carry outside jobs, and 63% of Wisconsin's volunteer-staffed agencies don't provide continuous 24/7/365 coverage at all. This model now combines both: P(uncovered) = 1 − P(truck free) × P(crew assembles), where the crew-assembly term is driven by time-of-day/weekday (workday hours = fewer volunteers reachable) and roster size. The per-volunteer availability numbers behind that term are a labeled assumption, not a fitted or observed quantity — no public per-county roster-response dataset exists. Closing that gap is the single highest-value next step, exactly as accurate to say here as it was for the original pooling method in FINDINGS.md.

Honest framing

FAQ: critiques, and how this product responds to them

Every question below is a real objection this project was tested against, not a hypothetical. Where the objection is right, we say so and show what changed. Where it's complicated, we show the actual research rather than argue around it.

The data and the model

Is the data in this dashboard real?

Partly. Real: county population, land area, and coordinates (US Census); 3 years of history plus a 16-day forecast of actual weather at each county's location (Open-Meteo); EMS response rates by region (California EMSA's CY2020 report); ambulance structure for 2 of 19 counties (verified from public agency pages).

Modeled, not observed: the daily/hourly call counts themselves, transport durations, ambulance fleet size for 17 of 19 counties, and the crew-assembly model. No county publishes a public per-day dispatch log, so there's nothing to fit call counts to directly. See the Data Provenance panel above and data/DATA_SOURCES.md for the full breakdown.

How do you know the statistical method (partial pooling) actually works?

Two different validations, because you can't check a new method against real data that barely exists yet:

  • Simulated data with known ground truth (pooling_experiment.py, temporal_pooling.py, sensitivity_sweep.py): generate data from a known process, check whether partial pooling recovers it better than the alternatives, and map exactly when it helps vs. hurts. It ties county-only estimation and beats naive lumping by a modest, honestly-reported margin — not a rigged win.
  • Real, heterogeneous inputs (this app's 19 real counties): swapping in real weather and real population actually caught two real bugs (an intercept-mismatch in the pooling math, and a tiering bug), which were found and fixed. That's evidence the pipeline is robust under real conditions, not evidence it's shaky.

What's not yet validated: whether the modeled call volumes match a specific real county's actual calls day-to-day. That needs real dispatch data from a pilot county.

Why does a county sometimes show "High" risk almost every day that week?

The Low/Below avg/Typical/Elevated/High label uses a fixed scale, the same for every county (under 3% / 3–10% / 10–20% / 20–35% / over 35%) — it's not graded on a curve against that county's own week. If a county's real weather and thin volunteer roster put it above 35% most days, it'll show "High" most days. That's a genuine, if uncomfortable, reading of the inputs, not a display bug — and it's more honest than a relative scale that would quietly relabel a bad week as "average for you."

"Does faster response even save lives?"

Doesn't research show ambulance response time barely affects survival?

Yes, and this checks out under scrutiny. A systematic review and meta-analysis covering 115 studies and 691,056 patients found "insufficient evidence to infer an association between EMS response time and patient survival," with very low certainty under GRADE. A widely-cited trauma study (Pons et al.) found no survival difference from an 8-minute response after controlling for severity — benefit only appeared within 4 minutes for higher-risk patients.

Why this doesn't kill the idea, but does narrow it: every one of those studies is urban, comparing 5 vs. 10 minutes. None studies the rural failure mode, which usually isn't a slow arrival — it's a call going unanswered entirely. Nobody has measured the gap between "someone came" and "nobody came," because urban systems rarely have that gap. That's why this product's outcome metric is framed as coverage gaps and crew-assembly failures prevented, not "minutes shaved" — the literature doesn't support the second claim, but it also doesn't address the first.

"Isn't the real problem staffing, not information?"

The volunteer shortage is the real crisis — doesn't that mean this doesn't help?

The shortage is real and well-documented: ~53% of rural EMS agencies are volunteer-staffed; more than 70% report difficulty finding volunteers; volunteer firefighter numbers are down ~25% since 1984 while call volume roughly tripled; 63% of Wisconsin's volunteer-staffed agencies don't provide continuous 24/7/365 coverage; 55% of rural agencies have 6 or fewer people providing 80%+ of their staffing.

This product does not create volunteers or funding, and doesn't claim to. What it can plausibly do: help point a fixed, scarce pool of people at the windows that matter most (documented daytime/workday shortage patterns are real and structured, not random), make mutual aid proactive instead of reactive (routine, frequent, and measurable — one mid-size county logged 48 mutual-aid activations in under 5 months of 2025), and reduce the number of calls in the first place through prevention outreach, which has the strongest real evidence of the three levers (see below).

With 1 ambulance and 10 volunteers, there's only one way to allocate that truck — how does this help?

It doesn't create a second truck. What it can influence, even with a hard 1-truck cap: who is specifically on call, when (a lot of rural gaps are "nobody was on call," not "the truck was busy" — see below), whether mutual aid was arranged before or after the crisis, and whether the highest-risk residents were checked on before they had to call at all. None of these require a second ambulance.

Isn't "ambulance already busy on another call" the wrong model for rural areas?

Yes — and this dashboard used to model it that way, via urban Erlang-C queueing math. Rural ambulance utilization typically runs 5–15% (vs. 30–50% urban targets), so simultaneous calls colliding is rare by construction. The real, documented rural failure mode is different: a crew doesn't form at all for the tone-out, because too much of a thin volunteer roster is unreachable at that hour (commonly because of outside day jobs).

The model now combines both: P(uncovered) = 1 − P(truck free) × P(crew assembles), where the crew-assembly term is driven by time-of-day, weekday-vs-weekend, and roster size. The exact per-volunteer availability numbers feeding that term are a labeled assumption informed by published research, not fit to any real roster — see the "Why coverage gap = crew assembly" panel above.

What evidence actually supports this working?

Is there real evidence, or is this just a modeling exercise?
  • Prevention/community paramedicine has the strongest evidence of the three action types — a rural Nova Scotia program cut annual ER trips 40%; a rural Ontario program cut 911 activations 24% and hospital admissions after ED visits 55%; a Washington program saw a 63% drop in 911 utilization among enrolled adults. One of these was a randomized controlled trial, not just industry reporting.
  • Demand-based deployment (System Status Management) is established EMS doctrine going back decades — though the literature itself is honestly split on how much it actually delivers vs. its reputation, which we're not going to paper over.
  • Scheduling predictability improves volunteer retention (self-scheduling and control over time commitment are documented retention factors) — a plausible, if modest, way this indirectly helps the workforce problem without solving it.
  • Mutual aid is standard, formalized emergency-management doctrine (FEMA's NIMS mutual aid guideline), and real counties activate it dozens of times a year — but its measured effectiveness specifically at closing rural coverage gaps is, honestly, thin in the published literature.
Has this been validated against a real county's actual call data?

No. Four counties in this dataset have a real, publicly reported annual call-volume figure we could check the model against (see the Model Calibration panel above) — the model runs 13–41% low against all four, reported as-is rather than corrected for. That's the closest thing to real validation available without a data-sharing agreement. A genuine pilot — a real county sharing dispatch/CAD data, held out and checked against the forecast — is the actual next step, the same way FINDINGS.md flagged real NEMSIS data as the highest-value next step for the pooling method itself.

So is this ready to show a county rep as "here's what your county's numbers are"?

Not as validated fact for a specific county — not yet. The methodology and mechanics are real and stress-tested; the specific percentages shown for any one county are modeled, not observed. The honest pitch: "here's exactly what this would show once we have your agency's real dispatch history — the engine and the three decisions it drives are real, the numbers are illustrative until then." That's a fundable ask, and it's a stronger position than presenting today's numbers as already proven.

Why is the prevention savings figure based on 150 people and not everyone flagged?

Because a community paramedic can only realistically carry an active caseload of roughly 150 patients. An earlier version of this page multiplied published per-person savings by every resident the risk model flags — which for a county like Mariposa meant ~1,890 people, about 11% of the entire county population, and produced a headline of $1.9–3.6M/year.

No real program enrolls a tenth of a county. That number would not survive thirty seconds of questioning from a county administrator, so it's now computed on a realistic enrolled caseload instead. The flagged count is still shown — it tells you how much unmet need exists and how much a second community paramedic could add — but the dollar figure only counts people a real program could actually serve.

How do you quantify how much this would save, and what won't you claim?

What we compute: an estimated count of 911 calls per year that arrive during an uncovered hour (see the Coverage tab's "Without this forecast vs. with it" panel). This isn't a new assumption layered on top — each hour's gap probability already is P(a call arrives AND goes uncovered), so summing it across a representative year of real weather is a direct expectation, not an extrapolation. For prevention, we show a dollar range drawn from published community paramedicine programs' documented per-participant savings ($1,000–1,900/ year), applied to the number of residents flagged — explicitly labeled as based on other programs' results, not measured for this county.

What we deliberately do NOT compute: a "lives saved" or dollar figure derived from converting a coverage-gap reduction into a mortality benefit (e.g. via a Value of Statistical Life calculation). The systematic review discussed above found insufficient evidence linking EMS response time to survival at the margins this product operates in — using that same weak evidence to produce a monetized lives-saved number would be exactly the kind of "rigged win" this project's own FINDINGS.md was written to avoid. An uncovered-calls-prevented count is honest and useful without needing that unsupported step.
