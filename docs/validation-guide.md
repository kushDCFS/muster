# Validation guide — who to contact, what to ask

The goal of these calls is **not** to sell anything or to hear that the idea is
good. It is to find out, as fast and as cheaply as possible, whether the
premise is wrong. Five phone calls can save six months.

Blue Ocean validation has a specific shape: you are testing whether the
**non-customers** have the problem you think they have, and whether the things
you chose to **eliminate** are things they actually don't need. Both are
falsifiable in a 20-minute conversation.

---

## 1. Who to contact

Start local and small. Large career departments do not have this problem — a
staffed station always forms a crew — so they will tell you the idea is
pointless, and they will be right about themselves and wrong about the market.

| **Newcastle Fire Protection District** | (916) 663-3323 | ~20 min away, small district, combination staffing |
| **Penryn Fire Protection District** | via penrynfire.ca.gov | Small, adjacent, similar profile |
| **Foresthill Fire Protection District** | (530) 367-2465 | Genuinely rural, long transport distances, foothill terrain |
| **Placer County Fire / CAL FIRE** | (530) 745-3000 | Covers the county's volunteer stations (Dutch Flat, Sheridan, Paige) |
| CAL FIRE volunteer program | (530) 889-0111 | Routes you to a chief who works with volunteer stations |

Ask for **the chief, an assistant chief, or whoever handles the annual report**.
That last phrasing works well — the person who compiles run statistics is
usually the person who has felt this problem most.

### Tier 2 — regional (one call reaches many agencies)

- **Sierra–Sacramento Valley EMS Agency (S-SV EMS)** — (916) 625-1702. The
  LEMSA covering Placer, Nevada, Colusa, Glenn and more. Already in your
  dataset. If they find this interesting, they can introduce you to a dozen
  agencies at once.
- **Western Placer Chiefs Association** — wpcfca.org/department-contacts. A
  room full of exactly your target buyer.

### Tier 3 — statewide / national (later, once you have 3 interviews)

- California Fire Chiefs Association — volunteer & combination section
- National Volunteer Fire Council (NVFC)
- California EMS Authority (EMSA) — they published the data you're using

## 2. How to make contact

**Call, don't email.** Small departments run on phones. Email to a general
inbox often dies. Tuesday–Thursday, mid-morning, avoids shift change.

### Phone opener

> "Hi, my name is ___ and I'm a student in Granite Bay working on a project
> about volunteer staffing and response times. I'm not selling anything and I
> don't want any money — I'm trying to understand a problem and I think your
> department would know more about it than anything I can read. Is there
> someone there who'd have fifteen minutes to tell me if I've got it wrong?"

Three things are doing work in that: **student**, **not selling**, and
**tell me if I've got it wrong**. Fire chiefs give students remarkable access,
and asking to be corrected is far more disarming than asking for validation.

### If you must email

> **Subject:** Student question about volunteer turnout times
>
> Chief ___,
>
> I'm a student in Granite Bay working on a project about why rural
> departments sometimes can't get a crew out the door — not response distance,
> but the gap between the tone dropping and the wheels turning.
>
> I've analyzed 65,000 real San Francisco dispatch records and found their
> engines meet the NFPA 1720 60-second turnout standard only 30% of the time,
> which surprised me. I'd like to know whether that's meaningful or whether
> I'm measuring the wrong thing entirely.
>
> Would you have 15 minutes in the next two weeks? I'm not selling anything —
> I'd genuinely like to be told where I'm wrong.
>
> Thank you,
> ___

Lead with the finding, not the product. It signals you did work before asking
for their time.

## 3. What to ask

Ask in this order. The first two can kill the idea, so ask them **before** you
have described what you built and before they feel invested in being nice.

### The two that matter most

**Q1 — Does the failure exist, and is it tracked?**
> "When a call comes in and a crew can't be assembled, what actually happens
> next? And roughly how often does that happen in a month?"

- *"Often, and honestly we don't track it"* → **strongest possible answer.**
  The problem is real and unmeasured. That is your whole thesis.
- *"It happens but dispatch handles it"* → real, but you need to learn who
  feels the cost.
- *"That basically doesn't happen here"* → this department isn't your customer.
  Ask who nearby it does happen to.

**Q2 — Is the information actionable?**
> "If you could fund a handful of staffed hours a week — not a whole position —
> would you already know which hours to pick?"

- *Hesitation, or "I'd guess Tuesday mornings but I'm not sure"* → the
  optimizer has real value.
- *"Obviously Tue–Thu daytime, I just can't afford it"* → the information isn't
  the bottleneck. Your product is the **funding document**, not the analysis.
  That is a pivot, not a failure — but you need to hear it.

### Testing the Blue Ocean choices

**Q3 — validates what you ELIMINATED**
> "Existing software helps decide where to position ambulances. With the units
> you have, is that a decision you actually make?"

If they say "we have one engine, there's one station" — you've confirmed that
eliminating placement optimization costs them nothing. That's your ERRC grid
validated in one sentence.

**Q4 — validates the buyer**
> "If a tool like this cost a few thousand dollars a year, where would that
> money come from — your budget, the county, or a grant?"

Listen for whether "grant" comes up unprompted. If it does, your funding-packet
thesis is confirmed by the customer rather than by you.

**Q5 — tests the data ask**
> "If someone asked you for a spreadsheet of call times — no patient
> information, just timestamps — how hard would that be to produce?"

- *"Ten minutes in ESO/ImageTrend"* → your onboarding is easy.
- *"I'd have to ask our vendor / the county runs dispatch"* → you need to plan
  for a screen-share, not an email request.

### The closing ask

> "Would you be willing to send me that export? I'll run the analysis free and
> send you back a document you can put in a grant application. If it's useless,
> I'd like you to tell me that too."

---

## 4. What to record

For each call, write down within ten minutes of hanging up:

1. Agency name, size, staffing model (all-volunteer / combination / career)
2. **Verbatim answer to Q1 and Q2** — exact words, not your paraphrase
3. Anything that surprised you or contradicted your assumptions
4. Whether they agreed to send data

Verbatim quotes are worth far more in a pitch than a summary. *"We don't track
it, but I can tell you it's most Tuesdays"* from a real chief beats any
statistic you can cite.

---

## 5. How to read the results

| Pattern across 5 calls | What it means |
|---|---|
| Most say "often, untracked" **and** hesitate on Q2 | Thesis holds. Build the optimizer, get pilot data. |
| Most say "often, untracked" but know their hours | The analysis isn't the product. The **funding packet** is. Lead with that. |
| Most say it rarely happens | Premise is wrong for this region. Ask them who it *does* happen to, and go there before concluding anything. |
| Nobody will talk to you | Not a signal about the idea — a signal about the approach. Try the chiefs' association or S-SV EMS instead of cold calls. |

**A negative result is a real result.** A pitch that says *"we called five
departments and two told us we were wrong about X, so we changed it"* is
dramatically stronger than one with no contact at all. Judges can tell the
difference between a team that talked to customers and a team that didn't.
